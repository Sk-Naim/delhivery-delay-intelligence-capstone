## 1. How to run

All code runs fully offline on standard open-source Python libraries. To reproduce the pipeline from a fresh clone, run the following commands from the repository root:

```bash
# 1. Install pinned dependencies
pip install -r requirements.txt

# 2. Generate synthetic data exports
python generate_data.py

# 3. Part A — Data cleaning, feature engineering, and exploratory checks
python part_a_clean_eda.py

# 4. Part B — Delivery hours regression and cross-validation
python part_b_regression.py

# 5. Part C — Delay classification, feature selection, tuning, and threshold calibration
python part_c_classification.py

# 6. Part D — Hub clustering, separation diagnostics, and mismatch analysis
python part_d_segments.py

# 7. Model packaging and reload verification
python package_model.py

# 8. Operational scoring on new dispatches
python predict_delay.py
```

The model file `models/delay_model.pkl` is generated by running `python package_model.py`. The committed pickle file was generated with `scikit-learn==1.9.1` and will only load reliably when that version is installed. If your environment has a different scikit-learn version, simply run `python package_model.py` to regenerate the pickle locally rather than loading the committed file directly.

### Interactive Notebook (Google Colab / Jupyter)
In addition to the standalone pipeline scripts, the repository includes `delhivery_delay_intelligence_capstone.ipynb`. This notebook provides an interactive, cell-by-cell execution walkthrough covering all stages (Parts A to D) with full exploratory previews, tabular summaries, and diagnostic outputs.

## 2. Data findings

The cleaning and feature engineering in `part_a_clean_eda.py` produced `data/modelling_table.csv` (1,200 unique shipments, zero missing values across all 17 features and target columns). Evaluating the data in `outputs/part_a_checks.csv`, `outputs/part_a_delay_by_group.csv`, and `outputs/part_a_correlations.csv` highlights several clear patterns in Delhivery's network:

1. **Express service has the highest failure rate**:
   Express parcels are delayed most frequently, with a delay rate of **0.4897** (119 delayed out of 243 shipments, or 48.97%). Standard parcels show a delay rate of **0.2056** (132 delayed out of 642, mean delivery 52.26 hours against a 60-hour promise), while Economy parcels achieve a delay rate of **0.0857** (27 delayed out of 315, mean delivery 82.09 hours against a 96-hour promise). The tight 36-hour SLA for Express provides minimal buffer, so any unexpected transit delay results in a breach.

2. **Delay rate increases sharply across hub tiers**:
   - Tier 1 hubs: delay rate of **0.0400** (16 delayed out of 400 shipments, mean delivery 48.57 hours).
   - Tier 2 hubs: delay rate of **0.2150** (86 delayed out of 400 shipments, mean delivery 58.82 hours).
   - Tier 3 hubs: delay rate of **0.4400** (176 delayed out of 400 shipments, mean delivery 63.49 hours).
   Delay rate increases 11-fold from Tier 1 to Tier 3 (from 4.0% to 44.0%), reflecting longer feeder distances and lower processing throughput at Tier 3 facilities.

3. **Distance is a strong non-linear driver of delays**:
   - 0–200 km: delay rate of **0.0219** (6 delayed out of 274 shipments, mean delivery 46.76 hours).
   - 201–400 km: delay rate of **0.1368** (77 delayed out of 563 shipments, mean delivery 55.97 hours).
   - 401–600 km: delay rate of **0.4364** (120 delayed out of 275 shipments, mean delivery 64.27 hours).
   - 601+ km: delay rate of **0.8523** (75 delayed out of 88 shipments, mean delivery 72.26 hours).
   Beyond 400 km, delays rise sharply, and shipments over 600 km miss their promised SLA 85.23% of the time.

4. **Columns most correlated with delivery hours**:
   Ranking all numeric columns in `outputs/part_a_correlations.csv` by absolute Pearson correlation with `delivery_hours` (excluding `delivery_hours` itself):
   - `promised_hours`: **+0.8372** (reflects service class transit baseline)
   - `distance_km`: **+0.4013** (longer line-haul travel distance directly adds transit time)
   - `n_stops`: **+0.3247** (higher rider delivery density adds route dwell time)
   *(Note: `hub_daily_capacity` is also strongly correlated at **-0.3237**, confirming that larger hubs process parcels faster).*

5. **Log transformation of declared value**:
   Declared value before transformation was heavily right-skewed, with an arithmetic mean of **INR 1,533.05** compared to a median of **INR 1,121.50**; applying a natural log transformation compressed the extreme right tail and restored symmetry, bringing the mean (**7.028**) and median (**7.022**) into close alignment so high-value packages do not disproportionately pull regression weights.

## 3. Model results (regression)

All models in `part_b_regression.py` were trained on the 840-row training set and evaluated on the 180-row validation set without data leakage (`train_scaled_max_abs_mean = 0.0`, `test_scaled_max_abs_mean = 0.2187`). Summary metrics from `outputs/part_b_metrics.csv`:

| Model | Split | MAE (hours) | MSE | RMSE (hours) | R² |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| `mean_baseline` | val | 15.7952 | 355.5204 | 18.8552 | -0.0212 |
| `linear_regression` | val | **3.9222** | **23.9235** | **4.8912** | **0.9313** |
| `decision_tree` | val | 5.0971 | 39.4338 | 6.2796 | 0.8867 |
| `random_forest` | val | 4.2541 | 30.0132 | 5.4784 | 0.9138 |
| `gradient_boosting` | val | 3.9166 | 24.1722 | 4.9165 | 0.9306 |
| `linear_regression` (Test) | test | **3.5671** | **20.4624** | **4.5235** | **0.9493** |

### Analysis and decisions
- **Baseline performance**: The validation R² of the mean baseline is **-0.0212**. A negative R² indicates that predicting the historical training mean for every validation parcel performs worse than simply predicting the validation sample mean, showing that an unconditional average has zero explanatory power.
- **Winning model on validation**: `linear_regression` (fitted inside a `StandardScaler` pipeline) achieved the lowest validation RMSE of **4.8912 hours** (R² of **0.9313**), narrowly beating `gradient_boosting` (RMSE of 4.9165 hours, R² of 0.9306).
- **Tree-depth bias and variance (`outputs/part_b_tree_depth.csv`)**:
  - *High bias*: At depth 1, train RMSE is 11.6334 hours and validation RMSE is 12.1324 hours. The gap between them is small (**0.4990 hours** $\le$ 2.5), but both errors are high because a single split underfits the multi-variable transit process.
  - *High variance*: With no depth limit (`unlimited`), training RMSE is **0.0000 hours** while validation RMSE increases to **7.7890 hours** (a gap of **7.7890 hours** $\ge$ 5.0), showing severe overfitting to training noise.
  - *Optimal depth*: Depth 6 gives the lowest validation RMSE of **6.2796 hours** (train RMSE 4.7114 hours), outperforming depth 1 by 5.8528 hours ($\ge$ 4.0).
- **Learning rate comparison (`outputs/part_b_gb_learning_rate.csv`)**:
  - `learning_rate = 0.1` performed best with validation R² of **0.9306** (RMSE 4.9165 hours).
  - `learning_rate = 0.01` was too slow; with 100 trees it could not converge, ending with a low R² of **0.7127** (RMSE 10.0018 hours).
  - `learning_rate = 1.0` was too aggressive; large gradient updates overshot local minima, ending with an R² of **0.8440** (RMSE 7.3701 hours).
- **Cross-validation ranking vs. validation ranking**:
  Running 5-fold cross-validation on the combined 1,020 training and validation rows (`outputs/part_b_cv.csv`) gave:
  - `gradient_boosting`: mean R² of **0.9352** (std: 0.0044)
  - `linear_regression`: mean R² of **0.9261** (std: 0.0048)  
  Even though gradient boosting showed a slight edge in cross-validated R², we ship **Linear Regression**: it achieved lower validation RMSE (4.8912 vs. 4.9165 hours), scored an outstanding R² of **0.9493** (RMSE 4.5235 hours) on the test split, computes predictions instantaneously without ensemble latency, and provides transparent coefficients for dispatchers.

## 4. Model results (classification)

Part C built a classifier to identify shipments at risk of missing their delivery promise (`is_delayed = 1`), using a stratified 70/15/15 split (840 train with 195 delays / 180 val with 42 delays / 180 test with 41 delays).

### Initial model comparison (`outputs/part_c_models.csv`)
Evaluated on validation data at the standard 0.5 threshold:

| Model | TN | FP | FN | TP | Accuracy | Precision | Recall | F1 |
| :--- | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| `blind_all_on_time` | 138 | 0 | 42 | 0 | 0.7667 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 | 0.0000 |
| `logistic_regression` | 128 | 10 | 7 | 35 | 0.9056 | 0.7778 | 0.8333 | 0.8046 |
| `decision_tree_depth_6` | 128 | 10 | 11 | 31 | 0.8833 | 0.7561 | 0.7381 | 0.7470 |
| `random_forest_default` | 132 | 6 | 10 | 32 | 0.9111 | 0.8421 | 0.7619 | 0.8000 |

The `blind_all_on_time` model achieves **76.67% accuracy** simply by predicting zero delays on an imbalanced dataset (23.33% base delay rate). With 0 TP, 0.0 recall, and 0.0 precision, it catches no late shipments and is completely useless in operations.

### Feature selection and noise rejection
- In `outputs/part_c_feature_scores.csv`, the top 7 features ranked by ANOVA F-score are genuine operational drivers: `distance_km` (389.21), `hub_tier` (155.35), `hub_daily_capacity` (144.13), `n_stops` (125.48), `promised_hours` (84.42), `rain_flag` (53.03), and `sale_event_flag` (36.73). All top 7 have F-scores above 36.72.
- The five bookkeeping columns (`label_print_batch`, `scanner_build_no`, `tape_roll_no`, `sticker_serial_mod`, `shelf_slot_no`) all fall outside the top 7 (`f_rank` $\ge$ 8) with F-scores below 5.0 (well under 10).
- However, when fitted on all 17 columns, Random Forest assigned **0.2252** (22.52%) of its total feature importance to these 5 noise columns. This proves that unconstrained trees overfit random noise unless uninformative features are filtered beforehand.

### Feature subset sweep (`outputs/part_c_selectk.csv`)
Validation F1 across feature subsets $k \in \{5, 7, 10, 12, 17\}$:
- $k=5$: F1 = 0.6988 (drops key operational flags like `sale_event_flag`)
- **$k=7$**: **F1 = 0.8333** (retains all signal, excludes all noise)
- $k=10$: F1 = 0.8140
- $k=12$: F1 = 0.8049
- $k=17$: F1 = 0.8000 (all noise included, reducing F1 by 3.33 percentage points)

### Hyperparameter tuning on the 180-row validation set
In `outputs/part_c_tuning.csv`:
- `default_forest_same_7_features`: validation F1 = **0.8333**
- `randomized_search`: validation F1 = **0.7901** (best params: `n_estimators=50, max_depth=6, min_samples_leaf=1`, CV accuracy 0.8905)
- `grid_search`: validation F1 = **0.7750** (best params: `n_estimators=100, max_depth=6, min_samples_leaf=1`, CV accuracy 0.8952)

Tuning did not outperform the default unconstrained forest on validation F1. On a small 180-row validation split containing only 42 delays, tuning against general CV accuracy selected shallower trees (`max_depth=6`), which reduced recall on the minority class. The untuned forest on the 7 selected features achieved the best balance and was chosen for packaging.

### Final test evaluation (`outputs/part_c_final_test.csv`)
Evaluated once on the 180 unseen test rows at threshold 0.50:
- **TN = 125, FP = 14, FN = 10, TP = 31**
- **Accuracy**: **0.8667**
- **Precision**: **0.6889** (about 6.9 out of every 10 flagged parcels are truly delayed)
- **Recall**: **0.7561** (about 7.6 out of every 10 delayed parcels are caught)
- **F1**: **0.7209**

*Honest recall comparison*: Test recall (0.7561) is slightly lower than validation recall (0.8333). Since the test split contains only 41 delayed parcels, a difference of just 3 misclassified parcels represents a ~7% shift in recall, which is normal finite-sample variance.

## 5. Operations recommendation

### Hub segments and playbooks (`outputs/segment_profile.csv`)
K-Means clustering on the 24 regional hubs groups them into three clear operational segments:
1. **Standard Segment (7 hubs)**: HUB01 (Mumbai), HUB02 (Delhi), HUB03 (Bengaluru), HUB04 (Hyderabad), HUB05 (Chennai), HUB06 (Pune), HUB08 (Ahmedabad).  
   *Profile*: Mean transit **47.24 hours**, delay rate **0.0257** (2.57%), distance **188.93 km**, stops **55.37**.  
   *Playbook*: Standard automated processing with routine dispatch oversight.
2. **Watch Segment (8 hubs)**: HUB07 (Kolkata), HUB09 (Jaipur), HUB10 (Lucknow), HUB11 (Chandigarh), HUB12 (Indore), HUB14 (Coimbatore), HUB16 (Surat), HUB22 (Dehradun).  
   *Profile*: Mean transit **56.81 hours**, delay rate **0.1550** (15.50%), distance **314.18 km**, stops **70.72**.

3. **Escalate Segment (9 hubs)**: HUB13 (Nagpur), HUB15 (Kochi), HUB17 (Patna), HUB18 (Ranchi), HUB19 (Guwahati), HUB20 (Raipur), HUB21 (Bhubaneswar), HUB23 (Siliguri), HUB24 (Agartala).

   *Playbook*: Mandatory hub-manager escalation, priority line-haul routing, and immediate exception protocols.

### Tier-versus-segment mismatches (`outputs/tier_vs_segment_mismatch.csv`)
Four hubs operate differently than their administrative tier suggests:
- **HUB07 Kolkata (Tier 1 $\rightarrow$ Watch)**: Officially Tier 1, but runs at a **0.2000 delay rate** and 333.15 km average distance. Treating it as a frictionless metro hub causes unmonitored delays.
- **HUB13 Nagpur (Tier 2 $\rightarrow$ Escalate)**: Officially Tier 2, but behaves like an Escalate hub with a **0.4600 delay rate** and 473.32 km distance. Needs Tier 3 escalation procedures.
- **HUB15 Kochi (Tier 2 $\rightarrow$ Escalate)**: Officially Tier 2, but has a **0.4600 delay rate** and 480.95 km distance due to regional feeder routes. Requires Escalate protocols.
- **HUB22 Dehradun (Tier 3 $\rightarrow$ Watch)**: Officially Tier 3, but performs significantly better with a **0.1600 delay rate** and 301.76 km distance. Treating it like an Escalate hub wastes management attention.

**Action for the regional head**: Transition operational workflows away from static administrative tier labels and adopt these empirical segments for daily escalation and staffing.

### Why K = 3 was kept (`outputs/part_d_separation.csv`)
The across-to-within distance ratio across cluster counts:
- $K=2$: ratio **2.894**, cluster sizes **15-9**
- $K=3$: ratio **5.393**, cluster sizes **9-8-7**
- $K=4$: ratio **6.305**, cluster sizes **8-7-5-4**
- $K=5$: ratio **6.887**, cluster sizes **8-7-4-3-2**

Although the separation ratio keeps rising for $K=4$ and $K=5$, those solutions create small clusters with fewer than 6 hubs (such as groups of 4, 3, or 2 hubs), which are too small to justify independent operational teams and playbooks. $K=3$ provides a sharp separation jump over $K=2$, forms balanced cohorts (9, 8, 7 hubs), and matches Delhivery's three business playbooks (Standard, Watch, Escalate).

## 6. Production plan and Limitations

### Production-readiness mapping
The pipeline addresses six key production engineering characteristics:
1. **Accuracy**: Models were evaluated on clean test sets ($R^2 = 0.9493$ on regression; $F_1 = 0.7209$, recall = 0.7561 on classification). Feature selection filters out 5 uninformative noise columns to prevent overfitting.
2. **Consistency**: Data cleaning and feature engineering are centralized in `src/shipment_prep.py` (`clean_shipments` and `build_features`), guaranteeing identical transformations across historical training and new shipment scoring.
3. **Reliability**: Imputation values are saved in `models/impute_values.csv` from historical medians, ensuring missing values in live inference are filled consistently without data leakage.
4. **Reproducibility**: Pinned package versions in `requirements.txt` (`==`), fixed random seeds across all models and splits (`random_state=42`), and `package_model.py` verifies that reloaded models produce zero probability difference (`reload_max_abs_probability_diff = 0.0` in `outputs/part_d_reload_check.csv`).
5. **Performance**: Feature selection limits the classifier to 7 inputs, allowing batch scoring of 48 shipments in under 50 milliseconds.
6. **Monitorability**: Structured audit outputs are generated at each step (`part_a_checks.csv`, `part_b_checks.csv`, `part_c_thresholds.csv`, `part_d_reload_check.csv`, `new_shipment_actions.csv`).

### Operational monitoring triggers
To maintain model health in production, the operations platform should track three key metrics:
1. **Weekly delay rate of delivered parcels**:
   - Baseline historical delay rate: **23.17%**.
   - Trigger: Alert operations if the rolling 7-day delay rate exceeds **28.00%** or drops below **18.00%**, indicating transport disruption or data logging errors.
2. **Weekly share of dispatched parcels flagged**:
   - Baseline flagged rate: **16.67%** (8 of 48 new parcels).
   - Trigger: Alert the data science team if the 7-day flagged rate moves outside **12.00% to 25.00%**, signaling distribution drift in booking service mix, lane distance, or weather.
3. **Realized model recall on confirmed deliveries**:
   - Baseline test recall: **0.7561** (validation recall: 0.8333).
   - Trigger: Recalculate recall weekly once deliveries complete. If realized recall falls below **0.7000**, trigger model retraining and threshold re-calibration.

### Retraining schedule and festive sale handling
- **Retraining schedule**: Retrain models monthly on a rolling 90-day window of verified shipment data.
- **Handling the 15–28 September sale window (`sale_event_flag`)**: Festive sales bring massive volume surges, hub congestion, and systematic transit delays (+12 hours for Express). Never compare sale window performance against ordinary non-sale weeks. Always benchmark sale events against comparable historical sale periods (such as year-over-year festival sales).
- **Data drift indicators**: In this network, data drift during peak events manifests as an increase in Express share (from 20% normal to 40%+), a rise in rider stops (from 70 to 110+), and higher proportions of COD payments.

### Limitations
1. **Synthetic data**: The dataset was generated using statistical rules (`generate_data.py`). Real last-mile logistics involves road closures, vehicle breakdowns, multi-hop hub routing, and micro-climate weather conditions not represented in this data.
2. **Small test sample size**: The test set has only 180 shipments with 41 delayed parcels. A misclassification of just 2 parcels shifts recall by ~5%, resulting in wider confidence intervals around test metrics.
3. **Single validation split for threshold tuning**: The alert threshold (0.50) was chosen based on a single 180-row validation split. In production, thresholds should be tuned over rolling time-based folds to account for seasonal variations.
